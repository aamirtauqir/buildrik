# 0002 — Learner moved from naming violations to judging designs

**Date:** 2026-06-21
**Status:** active (milestone)

## What happened
While reviewing a hi-fi topbar mockup I built, the founder — unprompted — raised two correct design objections:
1. "The full `Northwind / Acme / acme-main / Home` breadcrumb shouldn't all be in the topbar" — a prominence call.
2. "Should Publish be in the topbar, or elsewhere?" — and implicitly noticed the topbar had no Publish while **Ask AI** was styled as the blue hero.

The second is a **real error in my work**: I had made Ask AI the primary button. The product's purpose is to publish a site, so Publish should be the hero. The founder caught it.

## Why this matters
L1 taught *naming* violations; L2 taught *rating* them. This is the next rung: the learner **judged a design they didn't make and found a genuine flaw** — without a rubric in front of them. That's applied judgment, not recall. It's the first evidence the skill is transferring to real decisions.

## What I changed in response
- Corrected the mockup: Publish = blue hero (top-right), Ask AI → quiet icon, breadcrumb dropped the agency (`Acme → acme-main → Home`).
- Wrote Lesson 3 ("What goes where") around the two rules the moment revealed: **prominence = frequency × importance**, and **the loudest button = the product's purpose**.

## Consequences for teaching
- Raise the zone of proximal development: stop spoon-feeding findings; present designs (mine, competitors', the live editor) and ask the learner to critique first, then compare notes. Their critique now leads.
- Keep validating correct catches explicitly — the confidence to disagree with the agent is itself the skill (see [[0001-worked-examples-not-homework]]).
- Lessons should increasingly be grounded in the learner's *own* objections raised in chat, not pre-planned topics.
