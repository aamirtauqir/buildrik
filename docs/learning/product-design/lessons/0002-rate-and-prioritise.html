Lesson 2 · ~9 minutes

Naming was step one. Now: rate, then rank.

In Lesson 1 you learned to turn "feels weird" into a named finding. A list of named problems is still just a list. The skill that turns a list into a plan is rating each one (severity) and then ranking them (what to fix first). I did that on your real editor below — three findings I left on the table after today's redesign.

The one move this lesson teaches
severity = how often × how much it hurts × does it block recovery. Then rank by severity — and break ties by effort.

How to put a number on it

Severity isn't a vibe. It's three questions, multiplied:

AskLow → High
Frequency — how many users hit it, how often?rare edge case → every user, every session
Impact — how bad when they hit it?mild annoyance → loses work / wrong outcome
Persistence — can they get past it, or are they stuck?shrug and move on → blocked, no way back
0 not a problem · 1 cosmetic · 2 minor (fix soon) · 3 major (fix) · 4 catastrophe (fix before anything else)

Rule of thumb: high on all three = 4. High frequency but low impact = 2. Low frequency but blocks recovery = still a 3.

Three findings from your editor — rated with the why

Finding A — Topbar, any site. The breadcrumb reads Demo AI Site › Home — site and page, but no agency or client above it.
· Match the real world + Visibility of system status
· 2 — why: frequency is high (every editor load), impact is high for an agency (you manage many clients; you can't tell whose site you're editing), but persistence is low (the canvas content usually reminds you). High×High×Low → a solid 2, and it climbs to 3 the day someone edits the wrong client's site.
· Fix: widen the breadcrumb to Agency › Client › Site › Page when that data is loaded.
Finding B — Topbar, solo draft. + Invite sits as a first-class button next to Ask AI, on a site with one editor and no content yet.
· Aesthetic & minimalist design
· 1 — why: frequency high (always visible), but impact is low (it's just visual noise, harms nothing) and persistence is none (ignore it). High×Low×None → a 1. Real, but cosmetic.
· Fix: demote Invite into an overflow / secondary spot until there's a reason to collaborate.
Finding C — Canvas, first load. A small 0 / 7 done pill floats bottom-right. It never says done what.
· Recognition rather than recall
· 2 — why: frequency high (every new user), impact medium (they must recall/guess it's the setup checklist instead of recognising it), persistence low (clicking reveals it). High×Medium×Low → 2.
· Fix: label it — Setup · 0/7 — or a one-line tooltip on hover.

Now rank them — and notice the tie-break

Two of these are severity 2 (A and C) and one is a 1 (B). Severity-first says A and C beat B. But A and C tie — so break the tie by effort:

#FindingSevEffort
1C — label the 0/7 pill2tiny (one string)
2A — agency › client breadcrumb2bigger (needs the data wired in)
3B — demote + Invite1small

A cheap severity-2 beats an expensive severity-2. That's the whole game: severity sets the order, effort breaks ties. Never let a 5-minute fix wait behind a 2-day one of the same severity.

The new tool: "expected result in one line"

You said features "have no expected result." Here's the test. For any control, write the one line a user would expect before they click:

Click 0 / 7 done → I expect: "the setup checklist opens."
Click + Invite → I expect: "a box to add a teammate's email."

If you can't write that line, or the real result doesn't match it — that's your finding, already half-named. Run this on five controls in your editor and you'll have five findings before lunch.
