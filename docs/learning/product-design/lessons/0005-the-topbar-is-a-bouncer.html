Lesson 5 · ~7 minutes

The topbar is a bouncer. Default answer: no.

You asked "what else can we add to the topbar?" — and that's the exact instinct that builds a cluttered product. A designer flips the question: not "what can I add" but "does this earn a place here?" The topbar is your most expensive real estate. Treat it like a bouncer who says no by default, and makes every feature earn its way in.

The earns-its-way-in test — three questions, all must be yes:
1. Is it production-ready? · 2. Is it global (whole project, not one element/panel)? · 3. Is it always-needed or at-a-glance status?
Any "no" → it lives in a panel or the ⋯ overflow, not the topbar.

Why "default no" is the whole game

Adding feels productive; subtracting feels like loss. So products drift toward clutter — every team adds, nobody removes, and you get 16 controls in one bar (yours did). The only defence is a standing rule that the bar rejects by default. Then density can't creep, because nothing gets in without passing the test.

The trap inside your own question: collaboration

"We have collaboration — it should be in the topbar." Half-right, and the half that's wrong is the important half:

Part of the featureTest resultWhere
Presence (who's editing now)global ✅ · at-a-glance ✅ · ready ⚠️Topbar top-right — once it's ready
Invite / Sharerare ❌ on question 3⋯ overflow
Commentsnot always-needed ❌A panel, shown in review mode
The decisive question was #1 — ready? Buildrik's real-time collaboration is currently demo-only (a prior review found six serious bugs and said "don't ship multiplayer"). You never give prime real estate to a feature that doesn't work yet. Design the spot for presence; keep the feature gated until it's solid. A bar full of half-working promises is worse than an honest, smaller bar.

The deeper rule: subtraction beats addition

Your original complaint was "too dense, AI-designed." The cure is not a better arrangement of more things — it's fewer things. Every time you're tempted to add, first ask what you can move out (to a panel, to ⋯, to the bottom bar). A great editor isn't the one with the most buttons in reach; it's the one where the few buttons in reach are the right ones.

Carry this forward

From now on, every "should X go here?" runs the three-question test out loud. Most features fail it — and that failure is the product staying clean. When something passes, it goes through the build handoff (Lesson 4). When it fails, it gets a proper home in a panel.
