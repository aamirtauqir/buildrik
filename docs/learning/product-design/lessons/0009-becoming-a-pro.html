You have the craft. Three habits make it pro.

You asked the biggest question: how to become a good builder-with-AI ("vibcoder") and design like a professional. The honest answer: you already built the craft this session — auditing, naming, rating, placing, handing off, designing every state, building without breaking, verifying. What separates a competent maker from a pro isn't more craft. It's three habits the craft doesn't include — and they all point the same direction: away from your own taste, toward the real user.

The three pro habits
1. User-first — start from who/what-job/where-stuck, not from the UI.
2. Research — watch ~5 real users; let what they trip on drive the work.
3. Measure — define what "better" means, then check it actually moved.

That's real craft. Most people who say "I do product design" can't do half of it. But craft applied to your own guesses just produces confident wrong answers. The three habits aim it at the truth.

Habit 1 — Start from the user, not the screen

An amateur opens Figma and asks "what should the topbar look like." A pro asks three questions first: who is this person, what job are they trying to get done, and where do they get stuck? The UI is the last step, derived from the answer — not the first. This is "Jobs To Be Done": people don't want a topbar, they want to ship a client's site without fear. Design the topbar from that job.

Your topbar redesign was already quietly user-first: "an agency manages many clients → they could edit the wrong one → keep the client name visible." That sentence is a job + a fear. Notice you reached the right design because you started from the user's situation, not from "make it pretty." Do that on purpose, every time.

Habit 2 — Watch five real users (the highest-leverage habit there is)

This is the one you've been deferring, and it's the single biggest lever in all of product design. You don't need a lab or a budget. Steve Krug's method: give five real people (an actual agency operator, a friend who builds sites) one real task — "publish this page" — and watch them try, in silence. Where they hesitate, misclick, or say "wait, what does this do?" — that's a finding no amount of expert auditing reliably catches. Five users surface ~85% of the serious problems.

Expert audits (what you learned) find what violates rules. User tests find what actually confuses humans. You need both; you've only been doing the first.

Habit 3 — Measure: did it actually get better?

Before a change, write one sentence: "this is better if ___." After, check it. Sometimes it's a number (more sites published, fewer support questions); sometimes it's a re-test ("now 4 of 5 users find Publish without help, before it was 1 of 5"). Without this, you can't tell improvement from motion. A pro closes the loop; an amateur ships and assumes.

So — how to become a good "vibcoder"

Building with AI well is exactly the PM + designer split you've been practising:

  • Direct it like a PM — give the AI the 4-part handoff (what/where/don't-break/done) and the why (the user's job), not "make it nice."
  • Judge it like a designer — hold its output against the constitution and the states checklist. Reject slop; demand every state; verify with the five rungs.

The AI is a fast, tireless junior who has no taste and never says no. You are the taste and the no. That's the whole job.
